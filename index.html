DSC 106 Final Project

NYC Public Transportation

Exploration of the details, history, and effectiveness of New York City's MTA public transportation.

By: Nikita, Irina, Natalie ·

The MTA's history in New York City is rooted in the development of infrastructure that began in the 19th century. Despite decades of barriers, including bankruptcy and operational failure, New York City's MTA system is still the busiest rapid transit system in the Americas, carrying approximately 650 million riders annually. The following visualizations explore the history of the traffic of the MTA system, as well as recent key indicators of the effectiveness of the transit system today.

In order to create these visualizations, we used the following public datasets provided by the MTA: MTA Monthly Ridership, MTA Subway Hourly Ridership, MTA Pulse Survey Beginning 2025

Current Progress

As of now, we have started with the first part of our data storytelling which is comparing different types of public transit. For the "All" tab, the chart is stratified by transit mode and month, allowing the viewer to compare all modes for each corresponding year. While the viewer is given the ability to toggle between each category, the purpose of the all tab is to show that in every year across almost every month when subway system data starts being recorded, the subway has the highest amount of riders. This would be essentially in highlighting to show why so much money is being allocated for the 5 year MTA capital plan in comparison to all transit systems. Starting off with this plot allows for us to show the viewer why the subway system is the most essential form of transit, and later segue into larger takeaways. We also added a Peak Year button that jumps the slider to the highest ridership year for whichever agency the user selects. This makes it easy to compare how different agencies peaked at different times. We also added callouts that now appear automatically for notable years; for example, when a viewer selects 2020, a callout explains the COVID-19 ridership collapse and its scale.

Challenges

Some challenges include incorporating the storytelling of this project to give direct takeaways and ensuring that no information is miscommunicated. For example, even thought the nY Subway system was built in 1904, the data recorded from MTA only begins in 2018, while other systems like the B&T and MNR are recorded in 2008. This makes it seem like there is a large transition in 2018 where the NY subway system all of a sudden has the largest ridership, so a viewer not paying attention might assume that B&T has had the highest ridership in all the years even tho this might not be true. Other challenges would be making the visualizations more meaningful such as adding callouts for context (ridership would fall for all transit during the pandemic), doing research to add when certain policies were in place, and incorporating sentiment data that we have previously analyzed to show how policy actually affects riders in other ways than just raw numbers in participation. 2008 — Where it all starts

Before the subway data arrives

In 2008, the MTA recorded ridership for buses, commuter rail, bridges & tunnels, and the Staten Island Railway. The subway data doesn't appear until 2018 — but its absence makes what comes next even more striking.

2018 — The subway enters the picture

One mode dwarfs all others

The moment subway ridership appears in the dataset, it towers over every other transit mode. In 2018 alone, the subway carried over 1.7 billion riders — more than all other modes combined.

2019 — Peak ridership

The high-water mark

2019 was the subway's best year on record in this dataset. Then, in a matter of weeks, everything changed.

2020 — The collapse

COVID-19 and the 90% cliff

By April 2020, subway ridership fell 90%. Bridge and tunnel traffic dropped 65%. Commuter rail fell 95%. The MTA faced a $16.2 billion budget gap — and the subway, as the city's most essential artery, bore the largest burden of recovery.

A $68.4 Billion Bet

The MTA's 2025–2029 Capital Plan is the largest state-of-good-repair investment in its history. More than 90% goes toward rebuilding and improving the existing system — not expanding it. Here's exactly where the money is going — and how much of it is really about the subway.

The subway's share

Nearly half the plan is subway

Passenger Stations, Subway Cars, and Signals & Communications alone account for over $30 billion — nearly half of the entire $68.4B plan. No other transit mode comes close. This reflects the subway's role as the city's most critical and most deteriorated infrastructure.

Compared to last time

24% bigger than the 2020–2024 plan

The previous capital plan totaled $54.8B. This plan is $13.6B larger — a 24.8% increase. But the subway's portion grew even faster, driven by two aging car models (R62 and R68) hitting end-of-life simultaneously and Depression-era signals still running on eight lines.

Still not enough

$13.7B/year vs. $23B needed

JP Morgan estimated the MTA needs $23 billion annually to match private industry peers. This entire 5-year plan averages just $13.7B/year — barely half of what's needed. The subway is being kept alive, not modernized.

One expansion project

Everything else is repair, not growth

The only expansion project in the entire plan is the Interborough Express (~$7.2B). Every other dollar goes toward state of good repair — fixing what already exists. For a system carrying 1.7 billion riders a year, the city is running to stand still.

The L train

400,000 riders. No backup.

The L serves 24 stops connecting Williamsburg, Bushwick, and Canarsie to Manhattan. When the MTA proposed shutting it down for repairs in 2019, it triggered a citywide crisis. For many outer Brooklyn neighborhoods, the L is the only viable connection to the rest of the city.

The outer borough lines

No redundancy, no alternatives

The J/Z, G, and 7 trains are the only direct connections for hundreds of thousands of Queens and Brooklyn residents. Unlike Manhattan lines which overlap, these routes have no parallel service — cut them and riders have no fallback.

The backbone

4/5/6 and A/C/E carry entire cities

The Lexington Ave line (4/5/6) alone carries more daily riders than the entire DC Metro system. The 8th Ave line (A/C/E) isn't far behind. Reducing frequency on either doesn't just inconvenience riders — it paralyzes the economic engine of the entire region.

The domino effect

Cuts don't stay contained

When one line is reduced, riders don't disappear — they flood adjacent lines. Overcrowding cascades across the network, slowing trains system-wide and turning a single funding cut into a citywide delay event.
